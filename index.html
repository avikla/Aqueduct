Brief explanation

This Roman-era water system, constructed between mid 2nd century BC to the 1st century AC, relied solely on gravity. At its heart are three large reservoirs known as Solomon's Pools, located southwest of Bethlehem and north of Efrat. The total length is 82.5 kilometers.

Southern feeder channels

These pools were fed from the south by two main channels, each sourcing water from several wells (marked in red):

  • The Arrub aqueduct (green 41,308m) - It begins at the Arrub pool and includes three tunnels.
  • The Biyar tunnel (purple 5,012m) - It starts at Ein-Biyar with a long tunnel before passing through the Dahr-Baku tunnel.

Solomon's Pools

Solomon's Pools themselves are three large rectangular reservoirs cut into the bedrock about 5km southwest of Bethlehem. A 2018 archaeological survey of the Lower Pool (the largest of the three, roughly 179m long and up to 23m deep) found that it was originally quarried directly out of the limestone bedrock, with terraces and staircases cut into its interior walls. The architecture, together with the earliest hydraulic plaster found inside, points to a Roman-period origin for the pool.

The pools were repaired and enlarged several times over the centuries, most likely including a major Mamluk-era renovation around 1483 CE and a further refurbishment under the Ottoman Sultan Suleiman. Their last significant active use was under the British Mandate: between 1923 and 1935 they were fitted with a pumping and filtration facility and fully replastered to supply Jerusalem, until a flash flood in 1932 breached the walls. By 1938 the water was no longer used for drinking, and by 1940 the site had become a popular local bathing spot instead.

Aqueducts to Jerusalem

From Solomon's Pools, two aqueducts carried water to Jerusalem:

  • The lower aqueduct (red 21,447m). A longer route passes through two tunnels before reaching the Temple Mount. It remained in use until roughly 100 years ago.
  • The upper aqueduct (blue 14,735m). Positioned about 30 meters higher than the lower aqueduct.
    West of Bethlehem, it employed an inverted-siphon made of stone.
    Its exact path into northern Jerusalem remains uncertain, so two possible routes are shown in purple with bridge or inverted-siphon options.

Instructions for Use

How to explore the map above:

  1. Use the layers control (top right of the map) to show or hide individual lines, tunnels, shafts and points.
  2. Hover or click on any colored line, marker, or diamond for details and links to relevant articles.
  3. Use the search box to find a place, and the ruler icon to measure distances.

Map symbols
